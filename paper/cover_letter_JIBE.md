**To:** The Editors, *Journal of Industrial and Business Economics*

**Re:** Submission of the original article "Unconcentrated markets, persistent ties: incumbency in Türkiye's public IT procurement"

Dear Editors,

We submit the above manuscript for consideration as an original article in the *Journal of Industrial and Business Economics*. The manuscript has not been published and is not under consideration elsewhere, and both authors have approved the submission.

**Question and findings.** Market-level concentration indices are the usual first screen for competition problems in public procurement. We ask whether they capture the dependence of individual public buyers on their suppliers. Using 9,991 awarded IT contracts from Türkiye's e-procurement platform EKAP (2010–2026), we find that no product market is robustly concentrated. Buyers nevertheless return to incumbent suppliers far more often than market structure implies. Of contracts awarded by a buyer that had bought in the same market before, 45% went to an incumbent. A permutation null that fixes each buyer's demand and each firm's wins within product market, year and province predicts 21%. About a third of these contracts are annual re-tenders of the same service, and they account for most of the excess. Excess incumbency nevertheless persists for new needs. In a conditional-logit model of supplier choice among firms active before each tender, prior supply to the buyer raises a firm's odds of winning about 40-fold. A future-supplier placebo, benchmarked by simulation, shows that much of this persistence reflects stable buyer–firm matching, while the state dependence implied by switching costs is clearest in renewals. Incumbency is highest in health information systems and under the negotiated procedure without publication, where it jumped around the 2017 reorganization of hospital procurement. Incumbent-won tenders attract a single bid far more often than other tenders.

**Fit with the journal.** The paper addresses industrial-organization questions central to the journal. It relates market definition and concentration measures to effective competition, examines switching costs, aftermarkets and incumbency advantages, and considers the regulation of public procurement. It builds directly on the network-based analyses of procurement concentration published in this journal by Fountoukidis et al. (2023) and Pliatsidis (2024). We extend them by benchmarking the persistence of buyer–supplier ties against explicit null models and a supplier-choice model, and by separating renewals from new needs. The paper also provides the first network-based evidence on Turkish public IT procurement.

**Transparency.** All source records are public administrative data. We audited the keyword-defined scope by contract value, resolved firm-name variants, validated the title-based classifications on samples, and report every value-based result with and without the largest contract. Code and derived data are openly archived, with natural persons pseudonymized. We make no allegation of misconduct. The paper does not measure prices and discusses legitimate explanations of incumbency, such as the continuity of installed systems.
