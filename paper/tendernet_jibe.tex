\title[Unconcentrated markets, persistent ties]{Unconcentrated markets, persistent ties: incumbency in Türkiye's public IT procurement}

\author*[1]{\fnm{Furkan} \sur{Aksoy}}\email{furkan78177@gmail.com}
\author[1]{\fnm{Arda} \sur{Şimşek}}\email{simsekarda5803@gmail.com}

\affil*[1]{\orgdiv{Department of Software Engineering}, \orgname{Maltepe University}, \orgaddress{\city{Istanbul}, \country{Türkiye}}}

\abstract{Market-level concentration indices are the standard first screen for competition problems, but they cannot show how often a public buyer returns to the same supplier. We study this with 9,991 information-technology (IT) contracts awarded on Türkiye's e-procurement platform EKAP between 2010 and 2026. By contract counts, supply is unconcentrated in every product market. Yet 45\% of contracts awarded by a buyer that had bought in the same market before go to an incumbent supplier. That is 1.7 times the rate under the most conservative permutation null, which holds each buyer's demand and each firm's wins fixed within market, year, and the provinces of both parties. About a third of these contracts renew recurring services, and they carry most of the excess. To ask how much of this persistence reflects switching costs, we model who wins among firms active in the market before each tender. We compare the advantage of past suppliers with that of future suppliers (a lead--lag placebo) and benchmark both against simulations of pure state dependence and pure heterogeneity. Neither pure mechanism fits. Persistence is dominated by stable buyer--firm heterogeneity, which may reflect fit, relationships, or favouritism; a state-dependence component consistent with switching costs appears only in renewals. Incumbent wins coincide with more single bidding and, in a sample of 143 tenders, with smaller discounts on the estimated cost, although that difference is not statistically significant. Buyer-level persistence, benchmarked against null models, is a useful screen alongside market concentration.}

\keywords{Public procurement, Incumbency, Switching costs, State dependence, Market concentration, Türkiye}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Competition authorities and procurement regulators usually begin with market structure. The Herfindahl--Hirschman index (HHI) and concentration ratios summarize how supply is shared among the firms in a relevant market, and fixed thresholds turn them into a first judgement about competitive risk \citep{hirschman1964,rhoades1993,HMG1992,MergerGuidelines2023}. These measures cannot show whether individual buyers keep returning to the same firm. In public IT, where installed systems create switching costs, such persistence is the natural worry: once a buyer has a system in place, competition for its later purchases can be weak even when the market as a whole is competitive \citep{Klemperer1987,FarrellShapiro1988,Shapiro1995,BorensteinMackieMasonNetz2000,FarrellKlemperer2007,Greenstein1993}.

Persistence, however, is not the same as lock-in, by which we mean persistence caused by switching costs. A buyer may return to a supplier because switching is costly (state dependence), or because that supplier simply suits its needs, location, or systems, or because the two have a relationship that the buyer values or that favours the supplier (stable heterogeneity). Separating these explanations is a classic problem \citep{HeckmanBorjas1980,Heckman1981,Keane1997,Dube2010}, and it matters for policy. Switching costs call for remedies that lower them. Heterogeneity may be benign, as with specialization and good matches, or it may reflect favouritism, which calls for scrutiny of discretion.

This paper asks how much buyer--supplier persistence there is in Türkiye's public IT procurement, and how much of it looks like state dependence. We reconstruct 9,991 awarded IT contracts from the national e-procurement platform EKAP (2010--2026), linking 2,814 firms to 2,890 buyers. The analysis proceeds in four steps.
\begin{itemize}
\item First, supply is unconcentrated. Count-based HHIs are below 1,000 in every product market, so a count-based concentration screen would raise no flag.
\item Second, persistence is large. Of the contracts awarded by a buyer that had bought in the same market before, 45.2\% went to an incumbent. That is 1.7 times the rate under the most conservative permutation null, which keeps each buyer's demand and each firm's wins fixed within product market, year, and the provinces of both parties.
\item Third, about a third of these contracts renew a recurring service, and they carry most of the excess.
\item Fourth, we model which firm wins among those active in the market before each tender. We compare the advantage of past suppliers with that of firms that will supply the buyer only in the future, and benchmark both against simulated worlds with pure state dependence and pure heterogeneity. Neither world fits. Persistence is dominated by stable buyer--firm heterogeneity. A state-dependence component consistent with switching costs appears in renewals but not in new needs.
\end{itemize}
Persistence is highest in health information systems and under Article~21(b), the negotiated procedure for urgent needs, and incumbent-won tenders attract single bids far more often. In a sample of tenders for which we collected estimated costs, incumbents grant smaller discounts, but the difference is imprecise.

We contribute in three ways. First, recent work has combined concentration measures with network analysis of procurement markets, showing that concentration indices and centrality can point in different directions \citep{Fountoukidis2023,Pliatsidis2024,Fountoukidis2026}. We shift the focus from the structure of the network to the persistence of its ties, benchmark that persistence against null models, and decompose it with a lead--lag placebo and simulation benchmarks. This shows that persistence is mostly heterogeneity rather than lock-in. Second, we separate renewals of recurring services from new needs, which isolates where switching costs plausibly operate. Third, we provide evidence on Turkish public IT procurement, a market that has received little systematic attention, adding to work on political connections and construction \citep{Gurakar2016,GurakarBircan2019,KutlayYildirim2024}. We also document a value-based audit of keyword-defined EKAP data.

\section{Background}\label{sec:background}

\subsection{Switching costs, heterogeneity, and incumbency}
The HHI is informative only relative to a relevant market; pooled across markets that are not substitutes, it is mechanically diluted \citep{rhoades1993}. In procurement the relevant choice set can be narrower still. The switching-cost and aftermarket literatures show how a supplier with modest market share can hold substantial power over its installed base \citep{Klemperer1987,FarrellShapiro1988,Shapiro1995,BorensteinMackieMasonNetz2000,ShapiroVarian1999}, and the European Commission has warned of such lock-in in public IT \citep{EC2013AgainstLockin}. Observed inertia can reflect such costs or persistent heterogeneity in preferences and fit. The two are distinguished by how past and future choices relate, as in lead--lag tests \citep{HeckmanBorjas1980,Heckman1981,Keane1997,Dube2010,Shcherbakov2016}.

In procurement, incumbents can hold cost or information advantages that shape entry and bidding \citep{JofreBonetPesendorfer2003,DeSilvaDunneKosmopoulou2003,LiZheng2009,Saini2012,Hyytinen2018}, and limited competition in government purchasing is common \citep{KangMiller2022}. Buyer discretion and the people who exercise it matter \citep{BestHjortSzakonyi2023}. More discretion raises the share of repeat winners without necessarily worsening performance \citep{Coviello2018}, but it can also be used to favour connected firms, at a measurable cost \citep{Baltrunaite2021,Szucs2024,PalgutaPertold2017,BaranekTitl2024,DecarolisFismanPinottiVannutelli}. Relationships that develop with a buyer's tenure can sustain repeat awards \citep{CovielloGagliarducci2017}. Relational contracts can make loyalty to proven suppliers valuable \citep{Board2011,CalzolariSpagnolo}. Past performance legitimately informs supplier choice, although it may deter entry \citep{DecarolisPaciniSpagnolo2016,Butler2020}, and negotiation can be efficient for complex purchases \citep{Bajari2009}. Persistence is therefore not by itself evidence of harm; its consequences depend on prices and quality \citep{BandieraPratValletti2009}.

\subsection{Procurement as a network}
Contract-award records are naturally two-mode. Network studies of procurement have examined corruption risk and favouritism \citep{wachs2019corr,FazekasWachs2020}, collusion in co-bidding networks \citep{wachs2019cartel,ReevesLatourMorselli2017}, and the structure of buyer--supplier transactions \citep{Popa2019}. Integrity indicators built from award data include single bidding and the concentration of a buyer's spending on one supplier \citep{fazekas2016objective,FazekasKocsis2020,Fazekas2024GlobalPP}. \citet{Fountoukidis2023} show for the European pacemaker market that concentration-type indices can signal competition while centrality reveals dominant suppliers, and \citet{Pliatsidis2024} measures concentration in Greek procurement with network methods. Our object is the buyer--supplier tie rather than node centrality, and our contribution is to benchmark its persistence and decompose it.

\subsection{The Turkish context}
Turkish public procurement is governed by Law No.~4734 (2002) and administered by the Public Procurement Authority (KİK), whose platform EKAP publishes notices and awards \citep{alsac2017}; see \citet{BugraSavaskan2014} and \citet{Gurakar2016} on its political economy. Two grounds for negotiation matter here. Under Article~21(b), which covers urgency after unforeseen events, and Article~21(f), which covers purchases of goods and services below an annually indexed limit, the buyer need not publish a notice and instead invites at least three firms of its choosing. Law No.~7144 (2018) extended 21(b) to certain works \citep{Alyanak2022}. Politically connected firms have been found to win more often under less competitive procedures \citep{Gurakar2016,GurakarBircan2019}, and market capture intensified during the 2010s \citep{KutlayYildirim2024}. In the health sector, Decree-Law No.~694 of August 2017 abolished the Public Hospitals Authority and its provincial hospital unions and returned hospital procurement to the Ministry of Health and to individual hospitals.

\section{Data}\label{sec:data}

\subsection{Collection and scope}
We collected award records of IT-related tenders from EKAP by keyword search (13,418 records with an identifiable winner). EKAP search matches substrings of titles, so it admits tenders in which an IT term appears only incidentally. For example, the keyword \emph{ERP} matches \emph{tuz serpici} (salt spreader), and \emph{technical support} matches hospital staffing. An earlier count-based cleaning left 13,024 contracts, but a value-based audit showed that errors were concentrated among large contracts. We classified the 150 largest contracts by hand, applied ordered title rules to the rest, and inspected samples from every rule. Of the 13,024 contracts, 1,627 (12.5\%, holding 17.7\% of nominal value) are not IT, such as housing construction, vehicles, and cleaning services. A further 1,406 are ambiguous, such as data-entry staffing or building works with security cameras. The main sample contains the 9,981 unambiguous IT contracts and 10 contracts for IT-enabled call-centre services, one of which is the largest in the data (the call-centre / help-desk product market in Table~\ref{tab:markets} also contains help-desk systems and services classified as IT) (the 2025 national hospital appointment call centre); value-based results are reported with and without it. EKAP lists one winner per tender, so multi-lot tenders appear with a single winner (116 titles suggest lots); we found no framework-agreement call-offs.

\subsection{Entities, markets, and values}
Firm names were canonicalized by normalizing characters and stripping legal-form suffixes, with the merge groups of the 300 largest firms reviewed by hand. This reduces 4,788 recorded names to 4,389 firms, 2,814 in the main sample. Generic buyer labels that pool units across provinces, notably the Ministry of Health's provincial directorates, were split by the province recorded for each tender (34 labels, 732 main-sample contracts). Each contract is assigned to one of 14 product markets from its title and each buyer to one of 11 sectors.

To validate the market classification, two AI coders (different language models), blind to each other and to the rules, labelled a random sample of 300 titles. They agreed with each other in 89\% of cases (Cohen's $\kappa=0.87$). The rules agreed with their joint label in 79\% of the 266 titles on which both coders agreed, with precision and recall of 1.00 and 0.99 for health information systems but 0.6 or below for custom software and maintenance (Supplementary Information). These are AI rather than human labels. Values are deflated to 2025 TRY with the consumer price index. In the released data, winners that are natural persons or lack an explicit legal form are pseudonymized. Tables~\ref{tab:data} and~\ref{tab:markets} describe the sample.

\subsection{Estimated costs}
For a blind sample of 150 repeat-eligible tenders, 75 won by incumbents and 75 by other firms, we retrieved the estimated cost (\emph{yaklaşık maliyet}), contract value, and product codes (OKAS, the Turkish version of the EU Common Procurement Vocabulary, CPV) from EKAP's tender-detail pages. An AI agent read these pages in the authors' ordinary browser session at a slow pace; its input list did not indicate which tenders were won by incumbents. Collection stopped at 144 tenders when the site displayed a human-verification check, which we did not attempt to bypass. The EKAP contract values match our records within 1\% in 99\% of cases, and 143 tenders have both values in lira.

\section{Methods}\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Concentration}
For each product market we compute the supplier HHI ($H=\sum_i s_i^2$, 0--10,000) and the four-firm ratio from contract counts and real values. Because single contracts can dominate value shares, we also report value HHIs without the largest contract and with winsorized values, with bias-shifted bootstrap intervals. As reference points we use 1,000 and 1,800, the ``moderately'' and ``highly'' concentrated boundaries of the 1982 and 1992 US merger guidelines; the 2023 guidelines returned to 1,800 for highly concentrated markets \citep{HMG1992,MergerGuidelines2023}.

\subsection{Incumbency, renewals, and null models}
A contract awarded by buyer $k$ in product market $m$ on date $t$ is \emph{repeat-eligible} if $k$ awarded another contract in $m$ strictly before $t$, and \emph{incumbent} if its winner had won from $k$ in $m$ strictly before $t$. The incumbency rate is the share of repeat-eligible contracts that are incumbent. A repeat-eligible contract is a \emph{renewal} if the buyer awarded a contract in the same market 6--18 months earlier with a similar title (Jaccard similarity of at least 0.5 between normalized, stemmed title words); otherwise it is a \emph{new need}. In the blind AI coding of 60 title pairs, 35--40 of the 42 pairs above the threshold were judged the same service, but so were 6--10 of the 18 pairs below it. Some renewals are therefore classified as new needs, which biases the contrast between the groups towards zero.

To separate persistence from market structure, we permute winners across contracts within cells and recompute all statistics (1,000 permutations each). This keeps each buyer's demand and each firm's number of wins in every cell, and hence market size, specialization, and entry and exit, while randomizing only who wins from whom. Table~\ref{tab:null} lists the cells. N4 and N4b use each firm's home province (the province of most of its wins before the tender), which partly reflects earlier wins from the same buyer and makes these nulls conservative. With 1,000 draws the smallest attainable $p$-value is 0.001, reported as $p<0.001$.

\subsection{Who wins, and why}
The nulls randomize winners among all firms in a cell. A sharper benchmark compares the winner with plausible alternatives. For each repeat-eligible contract we take as alternatives all firms that won in the same market in the 24 months before the tender. We then estimate a conditional logit of who wins among them \citep{McFadden1974,McFadden1978}. This describes the outcome of participation and award together, not the buyer's choice alone, since in open tenders the lowest compliant bid wins. The covariates are:
\begin{itemize}
\item whether the firm supplied the buyer in the market before (incumbent);
\item whether it supplied the buyer in another market;
\item whether its home province is the buyer's;
\item its recent activity in the market, total experience, and experience with buyers of the same sector.
\end{itemize}
All covariates are measured before the tender, and standard errors are clustered by buyer. The use of AI tools in coding, validation, and data collection is described in Section~3 and the Declarations. Tenders won by firms not active in the market in the previous 24 months (entrants, 39\% of repeat-eligible contracts) cannot be represented in these choice sets; we report estimates with and without them.

To ask how much of the incumbent advantage is state dependence, we use a lead--lag placebo \citep{HeckmanBorjas1980}: an indicator for firms that will supply the buyer in the market only after the tender date. Under pure state dependence, future suppliers should be much less favoured than past ones; under stable heterogeneity, the two should be similar. Because the design itself makes future suppliers look favoured, we benchmark the observed moments against two simulated worlds (100 replications each). In the state-dependence world, winners are redrawn in chronological order from the fitted model, with incumbency recomputed from the simulated history; the other covariates are held at their observed values, and recomputing them as well is reported as a robustness check. In the heterogeneity world there is no incumbency effect, but a persistent random buyer--firm effect is calibrated to reproduce the observed share of incumbent winners among the 3,013 modelled contracts (0.69). We compare four moments across the observed data and the two worlds, separately for renewals and new needs: the past-supplier odds ratio, the future-supplier odds ratio, the ratio between the two, and the ratio of the odds ratios for the buyer's most recent and earlier suppliers. Intermediate worlds that mix both mechanisms are reported in the Supplementary Information.

\subsection{Procedure, bids, and prices}
We relate incumbency to the procedure with a contract-level logit with product-market, year, and buyer-sector fixed effects and standard errors clustered two-way by buyer and winner \citep{Cameron2011}, and describe its timing with an event study (Supplementary Information). Bid counts come from result announcements for a year-stratified random sample of 393 tenders (2012--2026, design-weighted) and a larger non-random sample of 1,194 software tenders from 2021--2026. In the estimated-cost sample we compare the discount, $1-\text{contract value}/\text{estimated cost}$, between incumbent and other winners by ordinary least squares with heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Supply is not concentrated}
Pooled over all markets, the supplier HHI is 39 by count and 149 by real value (92 without the largest contract). By count, every product market is below 1,000 (Fig.~\ref{fig:conc}); the highest are call-centre services (690, 74 contracts) and health information systems (431, 95\% CI 405--458). Value-based HHIs exceed 1,000 only in three small markets dominated by one or two contracts. Health information systems, with 2,189 contracts and 205 firms, is the one large market close to moderate concentration (value HHI 831, 692--985). The gap between pooled and market-level indices is mostly arithmetic dilution across markets (Supplementary Information). A count-based concentration screen therefore raises no flag, and a value-based one flags only three small markets whose indices are driven by one or two contracts.

\subsection{Buyers return to incumbents}
Of the 4,913 repeat-eligible contracts, 45.2\% went to an incumbent. The nulls in Table~\ref{tab:null} predict between 6.2\% and 26.4\%, depending on how much of the market's structure they hold fixed. Observed incumbency exceeds every null ($p<0.001$); under the most conservative, N4b, it is 1.7 times the null rate, an excess of about 920 contracts. The excess holds in every product market under N3 (Fig.~\ref{fig:market}; for call-centre services, with 19 eligible contracts, only at $p=0.03$) and, under N1, in every year with more than one eligible contract (Supplementary Information). It is robust to recorded instead of split buyer labels and recorded instead of canonicalized firm names. It is also robust to excluding call-centre tenders, multi-lot tenders, natural-person winners, or named-product licence and maintenance renewals, and to including the ambiguous-scope contracts. Among the 540 buyers with at least five contracts, 51\% have a main-supplier share above their own N1 null (39\% under N2) after controlling the false discovery rate \citep{BenjaminiHochberg1995}. The network of buyers and suppliers is only moderately modular once degrees are held fixed (Supplementary Information); what stands out is the persistence of individual ties, not segmentation.

\subsection{Renewals and new needs}
A third of repeat-eligible contracts (35.5\%) renew a recurring service. Their incumbency rate is 72\%, and they account for 57--63\% of the excess, depending on the null. New needs go to incumbents 31\% of the time, 1.6 times the most conservative null (Table~\ref{tab:null}), and even contracts with no similar earlier title go to incumbents 1.8 times as often as the province null predicts. The difference between renewals and new needs holds at every interval since the buyer's previous purchase (Fig.~\ref{fig:gap}) and in each of the eight largest markets. In health information systems, 87\% of renewals and 61\% of new needs go to incumbents.

\subsection{Who wins: state dependence or heterogeneity?}
Table~\ref{tab:clogit} reports the conditional logit. Among the 61\% of repeat-eligible contracts won by a firm already active in the market, having supplied the buyer before multiplies a firm's odds of winning by 42 (95\% CI 36--49), holding constant its recent activity, experience, and location. Including entrants in the choice sets gives a lower bound of 22. The advantage is largest for renewals (141) and under Article~21(b) (96), and smaller for new needs (21) and open tenders (34).

The placebo and the simulations show what this advantage consists of (Fig.~\ref{fig:worlds}). Firms that will supply the buyer only in the future also have a large advantage: an odds ratio of 54 (42--70), close to the 64 for past suppliers in this placebo model (which adds the future-supplier indicator, hence the difference from 42 in Table~\ref{tab:clogit}). A world with pure state dependence cannot produce this. At the fitted incumbency effect it yields a future-supplier odds ratio of 20, and 22 or 24 when calibrated to the observed share of incumbent winners or when all covariates are recomputed from the simulated history. It also predicts past suppliers to be about three times as favoured as future ones, far above the observed 1.2 ($p<0.001$). Part of the future-supplier advantage reflects firms that grow across all buyers: controlling for their future wins with other buyers reduces the odds ratio from 54 to 42. It survives counting only ties formed more than a year later (49), so it is not driven by lots or phases of the same project.

The subgroups differ. For new needs, future suppliers are at least as favoured as past ones (odds ratios 40 and 30), which is what heterogeneity predicts and the opposite of state dependence. For renewals, neither pure world fits: past suppliers are twice as favoured as future ones, far below the ratio of 8 under pure state dependence but above the 1.1 under pure heterogeneity. Renewals are therefore consistent with a mixture: strong persistent heterogeneity plus a state-dependence component that, in simulations of intermediate worlds, corresponds to an incumbency odds ratio of roughly 7 to 20. Recency does not discriminate between the explanations. The buyer's most recent supplier is favoured over earlier ones (the ratio of their odds ratios is 1.75), but heterogeneity alone produces the same pattern, because a displaced supplier has revealed a worse match.

\subsection{Procedure}
Incumbency is higher under Article~21(b) than under open tendering (72\% against 44\% of repeat-eligible contracts, a gap well above its null). Most excess incumbency nonetheless occurs in open tenders, which are far more numerous. Under Article~21(f), 35\% of repeat-eligible contracts go to an incumbent, 2.6 times the province null. The 21(b)--open gap rose sharply around 2017--18. The rise is concentrated among health buyers and coincides with the decentralization of hospital procurement, although the difference from other buyers is not statistically significant. The exact timing and cause are uncertain, and the Supplementary Information reports the event study and its caveats.

\subsection{Bids and prices}
Incumbent wins coincide with weaker competition at the tender stage. In the random bid-count sample, tenders won by an incumbent drew a single bid in 71\% of cases (95\% CI 61--80\%), against 32\% (24--42\%) for other winners (Fig.~\ref{fig:single}). With procedure and year controls, the odds ratio is 4.4 (design-weighted; 3.6 unweighted with a renewal control). In the larger software sample the odds ratio for a single valid bid is 2.0, and 2.7 with a renewal control. These are associations: incumbents may win because others do not bid, or others may not bid because the incumbent is expected to win.

In the estimated-cost sample, contracts won by incumbents were awarded at a mean discount of 12.1\% below the estimated cost, against 16.5\% for other winners (medians 8.9\% and 11.2\%). The difference is 4--6 percentage points with or without controls for renewal, procedure, value, year, and market. It is not statistically significant ($p$ between 0.11 and 0.22, $n=143$). The point estimate is consistent with incumbents extracting part of the buyer's switching cost, but a sample of this size cannot establish it.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Interpretation}
The market-level and buyer-level pictures differ. Many firms compete, and no product market is concentrated, yet buyers return to their suppliers far more often than market structure, timing, and geography imply. The decomposition qualifies the usual lock-in reading of this persistence. Most of it is stable buyer--firm heterogeneity: firms that will supply a buyer later are almost as favoured as those that supplied it before, which switching costs alone cannot produce at this magnitude. Heterogeneity has benign and non-benign readings that our data cannot separate. It may reflect specialization, compatibility with installed systems, geography, or good past performance \citep{Coviello2018,DecarolisPaciniSpagnolo2016}. It may equally reflect the anticipation of multi-stage projects, or relationships that favour certain suppliers, as the Turkish evidence on political connections suggests \citep{Gurakar2016,KutlayYildirim2024}. Switching costs are visible where theory predicts: in renewals of recurring services, where the incumbent advantage exceeds what heterogeneity alone implies. New needs show no sign of state dependence.

Incumbent wins attract single bids far more often. In a small sample, they also carry smaller discounts on the estimated cost. Both patterns fit weaker competition when incumbents are present. Both also fit purchases that only the incumbent can supply. Procedure is one channel of persistence, not its source: persistence is highest under Article~21(b), but most of it arises in open tenders.

\subsection{Implications for monitoring}
Indicators of buyer--supplier concentration already exist in the integrity literature \citep{FazekasKocsis2020,Fazekas2024GlobalPP}. What our analysis adds is a benchmark and a decomposition. Buyer-level incumbency can be compared with permutation nulls that account for market size, timing, specialization, and geography. It can be separated into renewals, where switching costs are plausible, and new needs, where persistence reflects heterogeneity whose nature needs case-level review. Such benchmarked indicators identify candidates for scrutiny, not violations. Where switching costs are the concern, lowering them through interoperability and data portability or separate procurement of licences and maintenance is the natural remedy \citep{EC2013AgainstLockin}. Where persistence reflects heterogeneity, the relevant questions concern how buyers use discretion.

\subsection{Limitations}
The evidence is observational. The decomposition rests on a placebo and on simulated benchmarks from one model class, not on a structural model with bidders. We observe winners, bid counts for samples, and estimated costs for 143 tenders, but not losing bidders' identities or quality, so welfare effects are not established. Scope, product markets, and renewals are classified from titles, and their validation uses AI coders; human coding remains desirable. Firm names were canonicalized without tax identifiers. Reverting to recorded firm names lowers observed incumbency (0.394), and reverting to recorded buyer labels raises it (0.462); the excess over the nulls persists in both cases. Multi-lot tenders appear with a single winner.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
Türkiye's public IT market is unconcentrated, yet its buyers keep returning to the same suppliers. Most of this persistence reflects stable buyer--firm heterogeneity rather than switching costs. Switching costs appear in the renewal of recurring services, where the incumbent advantage exceeds what heterogeneity implies. Market concentration is therefore an incomplete guide to competition in public procurement. Buyer-level persistence, benchmarked against null models and decomposed into renewals and new needs, shows where to look and what kind of problem to look for.
