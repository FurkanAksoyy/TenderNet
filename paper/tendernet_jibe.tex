\title[Unconcentrated markets, captive buyers]{Unconcentrated markets, captive buyers: incumbency in Türkiye's public IT procurement}

\author*[1]{\fnm{Furkan} \sur{Aksoy}}\email{furkan78177@gmail.com}
\author[1]{\fnm{Arda} \sur{Şimşek}}\email{simsekarda5803@gmail.com}

\affil*[1]{\orgdiv{Department of Software Engineering}, \orgname{Maltepe University}, \orgaddress{\city{Istanbul}, \country{Türkiye}}}

\abstract{Market-level concentration indices are the standard first screen for competition problems, yet a procurement market can be unconcentrated while many of its buyers keep returning to the same supplier. We study this gap with 9,991 awarded information-technology (IT) contracts from Türkiye's e-procurement platform EKAP (2010--2026), linking 2,814 firms to 2,890 public buyers. After a value-based audit of the IT scope, firm-name resolution, and deflation, count-based Herfindahl--Hirschman indices are below 1,000 in every product market, and value-based indices exceed 1,000 only in three small markets dominated by single contracts. Buyers, however, return to incumbent suppliers far more often than market structure implies. Of contracts awarded by a buyer that had bought in the same market before, 45\% went to an incumbent, against 21\% under a permutation null that fixes each buyer's demand and each firm's wins within product market, year, and province. About a third of these contracts are annual re-tenders of the same service, and they account for most of the excess. Excess incumbency nevertheless persists for new needs, and in a supplier-choice model that controls for firm activity and home province, prior supply to the buyer multiplies a firm's odds of winning by about 24. Incumbency is highest in health information systems and under the negotiated procedure without publication (Article 21(b) of Law 4734), where it jumped in 2017--18, and incumbent-won tenders attract single bids far more often. Buyer-level incumbency, benchmarked against null models, is a useful complement to market concentration in procurement monitoring.}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Competition authorities and procurement regulators usually begin with market structure. The Herfindahl--Hirschman index (HHI) and concentration ratios summarize how supply is shared among the firms in a relevant market, and fixed thresholds turn them into a first judgement about competitive risk \citep{hirschman1964,rhoades1993,HMG1992,MergerGuidelines2023}. In public procurement this screen has a blind spot. A market can contain many suppliers, none with a large share, while individual public buyers keep returning to the same firm. Market-level shares are then low, but the effective choice set of each buyer is narrow. This is the familiar logic of switching costs and aftermarkets: once a customer has installed a system, competition for its later purchases can be weak even when competition for new customers is strong \citep{Klemperer1987,FarrellShapiro1988,Shapiro1995,BorensteinMackieMasonNetz2000,FarrellKlemperer2007}. Public IT is a natural case, and \citet{Greenstein1993,Greenstein1995} showed long ago that installed base shapes vendor choice in US federal computer procurement.

This paper measures that gap for the public IT market of Türkiye. We reconstruct 9,991 awarded IT contracts from the national e-procurement platform EKAP (2010--2026), linking 2,814 firms to 2,890 buyers, and ask three questions. Is the market concentrated once it is measured carefully? Do buyers return to incumbent suppliers more often than market structure, timing, and geography would produce? And where, for which kinds of purchases, and under which procedures is incumbency strongest?

The answers are as follows. First, the market is not concentrated. Count-based HHIs are below 1,000 in every product market. Value-based HHIs exceed 1,000 only in three small markets, each dominated by one or two very large contracts, and in two of them the index falls below 1,000 once the largest contract is removed. Health information systems, the largest market, comes closest to moderate concentration. Second, incumbency is pervasive. Of the contracts awarded by a buyer that had already bought in the same product market, 45.2\% went to a firm that had supplied that buyer in that market before. We compare this with permutation nulls that reassign winners within cells while keeping each buyer's demand and each firm's number of wins fixed. The null rate is 6.2\% when cells are product market and year, and 20.7\% when they also fix the province. Third, a large part of incumbency is re-tendering: about a third of these contracts renew a service bought by the same buyer 6--18 months earlier, with an incumbency rate of 72\%. Excess incumbency is not confined to renewals, however. New needs still go to incumbents 1.9 times as often as the province null predicts, and 1.6 times as often as the strictest null. In a conditional-logit model of supplier choice with firm activity and home-province controls, having supplied the buyer before multiplies a firm's odds of winning by 24 overall and by 14 for new needs. Fourth, incumbency is highest in health information systems and under Article~21(b) of Law No.~4734, which allows negotiation without prior publication. There, the incumbency gap relative to open tenders jumped in 2017--18, when hospital procurement was reorganized. Incumbent-won tenders attract a single bid far more often than tenders won by new suppliers.

We contribute in three ways. First, recent work has combined concentration measures with network analysis of procurement markets and shown that concentration indices and centrality can point in different directions \citep{Fountoukidis2023,Pliatsidis2024,Fountoukidis2026}. We move from the structure of the network to the persistence of its ties, and we benchmark that persistence against explicit null models and a supplier-choice model. This separates relational persistence from both the arithmetic dilution of pooled indices and the sorting of firms by market, time, and geography. Second, we decompose incumbency into the renewal of recurring services and the award of new needs. This distinction matters for interpreting incumbency as lock-in and is rarely drawn in procurement data. Third, we provide the first network-based evidence on Turkish public IT procurement, adding to work that has focused on political connections and construction \citep{Gurakar2016,GurakarBircan2019,KutlayYildirim2024}. We also document a value-based audit of keyword-defined procurement data that is relevant to other users of EKAP.

\section{Background}\label{sec:background}

\subsection{Switching costs, incumbency, and discretion}
The HHI is informative only relative to a relevant market. Pooled across markets that are not substitutes, it is mechanically diluted \citep{rhoades1993}. In procurement the relevant choice set can be narrower still. A hospital that has installed an information system faces data-migration, training, and integration costs if it changes supplier. The switching-cost and aftermarket literatures show how such costs let a supplier with modest market share hold substantial power over its installed base \citep{Klemperer1987,FarrellShapiro1988,Shapiro1995,BorensteinMackieMasonNetz2000,ShapiroVarian1999}, and the European Commission has warned of this lock-in in public IT procurement \citep{EC2013AgainstLockin}. In procurement auctions, incumbents often hold cost or information advantages that shape entry and bidding \citep{JofreBonetPesendorfer2003,DeSilvaDunneKosmopoulou2003,LiZheng2009}. Buyer discretion can amplify or discipline this. More discretion raises the share of repeat winners without necessarily worsening performance \citep{Coviello2018}. Discretion can also be used to favour connected firms \citep{Baltrunaite2021,Szucs2024,PalgutaPertold2017}, and it can be sustained by relationships that develop with a buyer's tenure \citep{CovielloGagliarducci2017}. Past performance legitimately informs supplier choice \citep{DecarolisPaciniSpagnolo2016}, and negotiation can be efficient for complex purchases \citep{Bajari2009}. Repeated contracting is therefore not by itself evidence of harm. Its welfare consequences depend on prices and quality, and on whether waste is active or passive \citep{BandieraPratValletti2009}. The empirical questions we can answer are how much incumbency exists beyond what market structure implies, and for which purchases.

\subsection{Procurement as a network}
Contract-award records are naturally two-mode. Network studies of procurement have used this structure to study corruption risk and favouritism \citep{wachs2019corr,FazekasWachs2020}, to detect collusion in co-bidding networks \citep{wachs2019cartel,ReevesLatourMorselli2017}, and to describe buyer--supplier transactions \citep{Popa2019}. The integrity literature builds risk indicators from award data, most prominently single bidding and the concentration of a buyer's spending on one supplier \citep{fazekas2016objective,FazekasKocsis2020,Fazekas2024GlobalPP}. Closest to our question, \citet{Fountoukidis2023} show for the European pacemaker market that concentration-type indices can signal competition while centrality reveals dominant suppliers, and \citet{Pliatsidis2024} measures concentration in Greek procurement with network methods by product group. We share their premise that shares and network structure can diverge. We differ in the object of analysis, the buyer--supplier tie rather than node centrality, and in benchmarking it against nulls and a choice model.

\subsection{The Turkish context}
Turkish public procurement is governed by Law No.~4734 (2002), the scope and procedures of which are described by \citet{BugraSavaskan2014} and \citet{Gurakar2016}, and administered by the Public Procurement Authority (KİK), whose platform EKAP publishes notices and awards \citep{alsac2017}. Article~21 lists the grounds for the negotiated procedure. Article~21(b) permits it where a tender must be held urgently because of unforeseen events such as natural disasters or epidemics; in these cases, and under Article~21(f), the buyer need not publish a notice and instead invites at least three firms. Law No.~7144 (in force 25 May 2018) added to 21(b) cases with special features of construction technique or urgency for structural safety, which concern works rather than IT \citep{Alyanak2022}. Article~21(f) covers purchases of goods and services below an annually indexed monetary limit. Politically connected firms have been found to win more often under less competitive procedures \citep{Gurakar2016,GurakarBircan2019}, and market capture intensified during the 2010s \citep{KutlayYildirim2024}. These studies focus mainly on construction and on political connections. The health sector was reorganized during our window: Decree-Law (KHK) No.~694 of 25 August 2017 abolished the Public Hospitals Authority and its provincial hospital unions and returned hospital procurement to the Ministry of Health and to individual hospitals.

\section{Data}\label{sec:data}

\subsection{Collection and scope}
We collected award records of IT-related tenders from EKAP by keyword search and retained those concluded with an identifiable winner (13,418 records). EKAP search matches substrings of titles, so it admits tenders in which an IT term appears only incidentally. The keyword \emph{ERP}, for example, matches place names and \emph{tuz serpici} (salt spreader), and \emph{technical support} matches hospital staffing contracts. An earlier count-based cleaning removed 394 obvious false positives, but a value-based audit showed that the remaining errors were concentrated among large contracts. We therefore classified the 150 largest contracts by hand, applied ordered title rules to the rest, and inspected random samples from every rule. Of the 13,024 contracts, 1,627 (12.5\%, holding 17.7\% of nominal value) are not IT, including housing construction, mining overburden removal, vehicles, and cleaning services, and a further 1,406 are ambiguous, such as hospital data-entry staffing or building works that include security cameras. The main sample contains the 9,981 unambiguous IT contracts and 10 contracts for IT-enabled call-centre services. One of these, the 2025 call-centre contract for the national hospital appointment system (MHRS), is by far the largest in the data; all value-based results are reported with and without it. The ambiguous contracts form a robustness sample. EKAP's award list reports one winner per tender, so multi-lot tenders appear with a single winner. About 116 titles suggest multiple lots, and we found no framework-agreement call-offs.

\subsection{Entities, markets, and values}
Firms appear on EKAP under spelling and legal-form variants. We canonicalized names by normalizing characters and stripping legal-form suffixes, and reviewed the merge groups of the 300 largest firms by hand, including firms that changed legal form or name under the same brand. Joint ventures were kept separate. This reduces 4,788 recorded names to 4,389 firms, 2,814 of them in the main sample. Some buyers are recorded under generic labels that pool units across provinces, most notably the provincial health directorates of the Ministry of Health. We split such labels by the province recorded for each tender (34 labels covering 732 main-sample contracts) and use the recorded labels as a sensitivity check. We assign each contract to one of 14 product markets from its title and each buyer to one of 11 sectors from its name. A blind re-classification of 300 random titles agreed with the rule-based market in 77\% of cases (Cohen's $\kappa=0.74$). For health information systems, precision and recall were both 0.99. Values are deflated to 2025 lira with the annual consumer price index; results with the producer price index are in the Supplementary Information. Of the 2,814 firms, 302 are identifiable as natural persons (sole proprietors). In the released data, all winners that are natural persons or lack an explicit legal-entity form are pseudonymized. Tables~\ref{tab:data} and~\ref{tab:markets} describe the main sample.

\section{Methods}\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Concentration}
For each product market we compute the supplier HHI, $H=\sum_i s_i^2$ on a 0--10,000 scale, and the four-firm ratio, from contract counts and from real values. Because single contracts can dominate value shares, we also report the value HHI without the market's largest contract, the largest change from removing any single contract, and a version with values winsorized at the 99th percentile. Confidence intervals come from 1,000 bootstrap resamples of contracts within market; because the plug-in HHI is biased upward under resampling, we use bias-shifted percentile intervals. We also compute HHIs within calendar years. As reference points we use 1,000 and 1,800, the boundaries of the ``moderately'' and ``highly'' concentrated ranges in the 1982 and 1992 US merger guidelines, the second of which the 2023 guidelines reinstated \citep{HMG1992,MergerGuidelines2023}. These are merger-screening conventions, not measures of conduct. Finally, we decompose the pooled index as $H_{\text{agg}}=\sum_m w_m^2H_m+C$, where $w_m$ is market $m$'s share and $C$ collects the terms of firms active in several markets, to show how much of the gap between pooled and market-level concentration is arithmetic.

\subsection{Incumbency, renewals, and null models}
Let contract $i$ be awarded by buyer $k$ in product market $m$ on date $t$. Contract $i$ is \emph{repeat-eligible} if $k$ awarded at least one other contract in $m$ strictly before $t$, and \emph{incumbent} if its winner had won a contract from $k$ in $m$ strictly before $t$. The incumbency rate is the share of repeat-eligible contracts that are incumbent. A repeat-eligible contract is a \emph{renewal} if the same buyer awarded a contract in the same market 6--18 months earlier whose title is similar (Jaccard similarity of at least 0.5 between normalized, stemmed title words, after removing years, numbers, and generic procurement terms); otherwise it is a \emph{new need}. In a check of 42 pairs above the threshold, 38 described the same recurring service. Pairs just below it were mixed, so some renewals are classified as new needs, which biases the contrast between the two groups towards zero.

To separate incumbency from market structure, we randomly permute winners across contracts within cells and recompute all statistics. This preserves each buyer's demand and each firm's number of wins in every cell, so firm entry and exit, specialization, and market size are held fixed; only the assignment of winners to buyers is randomized. Buyer histories, and hence eligibility and renewal status, are unaffected. In null N1 the cells are product market $\times$ calendar year. N2 adds the buyer's sector, and N3 instead adds the buyer's province. N4 adds the winner's home province (the province where it won most contracts), and N4b adds both provinces. Each null uses 1,000 permutations with fixed seeds; with 1,000 draws the smallest attainable one-sided $p$-value is 0.001, which we report as $p<0.001$ when no permutation reaches the observed value. Subgroups are compared with the same permutations restricted to the subgroup. For buyer-level dependence we compare, for each of the 540 buyers with at least five contracts, the main supplier's share and the supplier HHI with the buyer's own null distribution, and control the false discovery rate across buyers \citep{BenjaminiHochberg1995}.

\subsection{Supplier choice}
The nulls randomize winners among all firms active in a cell. A sharper benchmark compares the winner with plausible alternatives. For each repeat-eligible contract we form a choice set of the winner and 30 firms drawn at random from those that won in the same market in the same or the previous year, and estimate a conditional logit \citep{McFadden1974}; random sampling of alternatives preserves consistency \citep{McFadden1978}. The alternative-specific covariates are whether the firm previously supplied the buyer in the same market (incumbent), whether it supplied the buyer in another market, whether its home province, based on prior wins only, is the buyer's province, and the logs of its wins in the market over the previous 12 months, its total prior wins, and its prior wins with buyers of the same sector, together with an indicator for firms without prior wins. Standard errors are clustered by buyer and confirmed with a 500-draw buyer bootstrap.

\subsection{Procedure and timing}
We model the probability that a repeat-eligible contract is won by an incumbent with a logit that includes the procedure (open and restricted procedures as reference), an indicator for tenders from 25 May 2018 and its interaction with 21(b), the log real value, the buyer's prior contracts, prior suppliers, and years of history in the market, and product-market, year, and buyer-sector fixed effects, with standard errors clustered by buyer and winning firm \citep{Cameron2011}. Because the timing of any change is uncertain, we also estimate an event study of the 21(b)--open gap by year, for all buyers and for health buyers with and without buyer fixed effects, using 2013--17 as the reference period.

\subsection{Single bidding}
Bid counts are not part of EKAP's award lists. We retrieved them from result announcements for a year-stratified random sample of tenders. In 393 main-sample tenders from 2012--2026 the number of bids is reported. We weight by the inverse sampling fraction of each year and report design-based intervals. A second, larger but non-random source covers 1,194 tenders from 2021--2026 whose titles contain the word \emph{yazılım} (software) and reports the number of valid bids.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Markets are not concentrated}
Figure~\ref{fig:conc} shows supplier concentration by product market. Pooled over all markets, the supplier HHI is 39 by count and 149 by real value (92 without the MHRS contract). By count, every product market is below 1,000; the highest values are for call-centre services (690; 74 contracts) and health information systems (431, 95\% CI 405--458). By real value, three markets exceed 1,000. Removing the largest contract reduces the value HHI of smart-city and traffic systems from 2,580 to 823 and of education technology from 1,062 to 752. The call-centre market stays above 1,000 on every value variant, but it consists largely of the MHRS contract. Health information systems, with 2,189 contracts and 205 firms, is the one large market with stable concentration close to the moderate range. Its value HHI is 831 (692--985) and its value CR4 53\%, and removing any single contract moves the index by less than 40 points. Within single years, where small samples inflate all indices, its median count HHI is 671 (597 after bias correction) and its median value HHI is 1,091.

The decomposition shows why pooled and market-level measures differ. By count, $\sum_m w_m^2H_m$ accounts for 27 of the 39 points of the pooled index, and the weighted mean of market HHIs is 179. Dilution across markets is arithmetic, so we treat market definition as a precondition rather than a result. Even with product markets defined, supply is at most moderately concentrated.

\subsection{Buyers return to incumbents}
Of the 9,991 contracts, 4,913 were awarded by a buyer that had bought in the same product market before, and 45.2\% of these went to an incumbent. Table~\ref{tab:null} compares this rate with the nulls. When winners are reassigned within product market and year (N1), the expected rate is 6.2\%. Conditioning on the buyer's sector (N2) raises it to 11.0\%, on the buyer's province (N3) to 20.7\%, on the winner's home province (N4) to 18.5\%, and on both provinces (N4b), under which 31\% of eligible contracts sit in cells with a single possible winner, to 24.2\%. The observed rate exceeds every null ($p<0.001$). The excess ranges from about 1,900 contracts under N1 to 1,030 under N4b. It holds in every year from 2011 to 2026 and in every product market (Fig.~\ref{fig:market}). It is also robust to recorded instead of split buyer labels, recorded instead of canonicalized firm names, dropping call-centre or multi-lot tenders or natural-person winners, and adding the ambiguous-scope contracts (Supplementary Information).

\subsection{Renewals and new needs}
A large part of incumbency is the re-tendering of recurring services. The median gap between a buyer's successive contracts in a market is about a year, and 35.5\% of repeat-eligible contracts are renewals. Their incumbency rate is 72.0\%, against 28.8\% under N3, and they account for 57--64\% of the excess, depending on the null (Table~\ref{tab:null}). Incumbency is not only renewal, however. New needs go to incumbents in 30.5\% of cases, against 16.3\% under N3. Even contracts with no similar earlier title at all go to incumbents 1.8 times as often as N3 predicts. Figure~\ref{fig:gap} shows that the difference between renewals and new needs holds at every interval since the buyer's previous contract, and that new needs stay above their null throughout. The pattern holds in each of the eight largest markets. In health information systems, 87\% of renewals and 61\% of new needs go to incumbents (N3 nulls 45\% and 38\%). In enterprise software the figures are 82\% and 23\% (N3 nulls 23\% and 7\%).

\subsection{Supplier choice}
Table~\ref{tab:clogit} reports the conditional logit. Having previously supplied the buyer in the same market multiplies a firm's odds of winning by 23.8 (buyer-clustered 95\% CI 20.6--27.5; bootstrap 20.7--27.9), holding constant its recent activity in the market, its overall experience, its experience with buyers of the same sector, and whether it is based in the buyer's province. Supplying the buyer in another market also helps (odds ratio 4.3), and a home-province match triples the odds. The incumbency effect is largest for renewals (odds ratio 56.7) and under Article~21(b) (68.0), but it is also large for new needs (13.9) and open tenders (19.5). If the home province is defined from the whole sample, the incumbency odds ratio is 15.2 (12.9--17.8). This definition is not preferred because it uses the contract being predicted.

\subsection{Dependent buyers}
The excess is not driven by a few large buyers. Among the 540 buyers with at least five contracts, the median share of the main supplier is 0.33, against a typical null value of 0.16 (Supplementary Fig.~S2). After controlling the false discovery rate at 5\%, 51\% of these buyers have a main-supplier share and 66\% a supplier HHI above their own null (39\% and 54\% under N2). A quarter award at least half of their IT contracts to one firm. Dependence is most pronounced among individual Ministry of Health hospitals and dental centres, 88\% of which exceed their null. Per contract, excess incumbency is somewhat lower among the largest buyers (0.34 against 0.45 in the smallest tercile). Because their null distributions are tighter, however, a larger share of large buyers is individually distinguishable from the null.

\subsection{Procedure and timing}
Incumbency is higher under Article~21(b) than under open tendering (72.3\% against 44.1\%), and the gap of 28 points exceeds its null gap in all nulls. Most excess incumbency nonetheless occurs in open tenders (61\% of the excess under N1), because they are far more numerous. Among 21(b) contracts, 90\% of renewals go to the incumbent.

The 21(b)--open gap changed over time (Fig.~\ref{fig:event}). Relative to 2013--17, when it did not differ significantly from zero (about $-7$ points for all buyers), it jumped in 2018, by 37 percentage points for all buyers (95\% CI 24--50) and by 43 points for health buyers. Part of the jump persists: from 2019 the gap averages 14 points above its 2013--17 level for all buyers and, within health buyers with buyer fixed effects, 24 points (10--38). The best-fitting break is August 2017 rather than May 2018, but only 18 repeat-eligible 21(b) contracts fall between the two dates, so the data cannot separate them. Two considerations point to the reorganization of hospital procurement rather than Law No.~7144. The 2018 amendment concerns works, and the change is concentrated among health buyers: in the contract-level logit, the 21(b) $\times$ post-2018 interaction has an odds ratio of 2.64 among health buyers and 0.86 among others (overall 2.42, 95\% CI 1.43--4.09; 2.93 without the buyer-history controls). The early years 2010--12, which have little buyer history, also show a high gap, so the comparison depends on using 2013--17 as the reference.

\subsection{Incumbency and single bidding}
Incumbent wins coincide with weak competition at the tender stage. In the random sample, 44\% of tenders drew a single bid (95\% CI 39--49\%): 75\% under 21(b) and 41\% under open procedures (Fig.~\ref{fig:single}). Tenders won by an incumbent drew a single bid in 71\% of cases (61--80\%), against 32\% (24--42\%) for tenders won by a firm that had not supplied the buyer in that market. Adjusted for procedure and year, the odds ratio is 4.4 (2.4--8.3), and the gap is present in open tenders (69\% against 37\%). In the larger 2021--26 software sample, 67\% of incumbent-won tenders and 49\% of others had a single valid bid (odds ratio 2.0, 1.4--3.0, adjusted for procedure, year, and market). Where the number of firms that obtained the tender documents is recorded, on average four firms did so but fewer than two submitted a valid bid.

\subsection{Network structure}
The network itself is sparse and only moderately modular. Communities in its giant component exceed a degree-preserving bipartite null by a small margin (unweighted modularity 0.73 against 0.69), align partly with buyer sectors and weakly with product markets, and brokerage across communities is largely a matter of firm size (Supplementary Information). The relational feature that stands out is not segmentation but the persistence of individual buyer--supplier ties.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Interpretation}
The two levels of analysis give different answers. Across the whole market, many firms compete, no product market is concentrated on robust measures, and pooled indices are very low. Within buyers, relationships persist far beyond what market structure, timing, and geography imply. The findings are consistent: market shares describe how supply is spread across all buyers, whereas incumbency describes how narrow each buyer's choice is. A market in which each buyer is tied to a different supplier has low concentration and high dependence at once, the configuration the aftermarket literature describes \citep{Shapiro1995,BorensteinMackieMasonNetz2000}.

The main mechanisms predict different patterns, and the data speak to some of them. Switching costs from installed systems predict incumbency concentrated in renewals and in system-specific products. This is what we observe: renewals carry most of the excess, and incumbency is highest in hospital information systems and maintenance services. Information advantages and past performance \citep{Coviello2018,DecarolisPaciniSpagnolo2016} predict incumbency for new needs as well, which we also find, although less strongly. An erosion of competition predicts fewer bids when incumbents win, and incumbent-won tenders do attract single bids far more often. The single-bid gap is consistent with incumbency advantages that discourage entry, but also with purchases that only the incumbent can supply, and we cannot tell whether other firms were deterred or uninterested. Distinguishing these mechanisms would require prices or estimated costs, which our data do not contain.

Procedure is one channel, not the source. Article~21(b), formally an urgency ground, has the highest incumbency, and 90\% of its renewals go to the incumbent. Its gap to open tendering widened after the 2017 hospital reorganization, when individual hospitals took over procurement. Yet most excess incumbency occurs in open tenders, and new needs show excess incumbency under every procedure.

\subsection{Implications for monitoring}
Procurement monitoring relies on market-level statistics and on red flags such as single bidding and a buyer's spending share with one supplier \citep{fazekas2016objective,FazekasKocsis2020,Fazekas2024GlobalPP}. Indicators of the last kind already exist. What our analysis adds is a benchmark: buyer-level incumbency and dependence can be compared with permutation nulls that account for market size, timing, specialization, and geography, and split into renewals and new needs. Such benchmarked indicators identify candidates for review, above all hospital information systems and renewals under Article~21(b). Whether dependence is harmful cannot be read from these indicators alone. Where switching costs are real, measures that lower them may do more for competition than additional tendering, for example data-portability and interoperability requirements for health information systems or separate procurement of licences and maintenance \citep{EC2013AgainstLockin}.

\subsection{Limitations}
The analysis is descriptive. We observe winners, bid counts for samples, and award values, but not losing bidders, estimated costs, or quality, so we cannot measure the price or welfare consequences of incumbency. The bid-count samples are small or non-random and start in 2012. Scope, product markets, and renewals are classified from titles. The classifications were validated on samples, but the validation labels for markets and renewals were produced with AI assistance and have not been checked by independent human coders. Firm names were canonicalized without tax identifiers, and splitting pooled buyer labels by province is an approximation. Reverting to recorded firm names lowers observed incumbency (0.394, 8.4 times the N1 null), reverting to recorded buyer labels raises it (0.462, 4.4 times), and the excess persists in both cases. Multi-lot tenders appear with a single winner. Finally, the timing evidence cannot separate the 2017 health reorganization from the 2018 legal amendment or from other changes around that time.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
Türkiye's public IT market is unconcentrated by conventional measures, yet its buyers are frequently captive to incumbent suppliers. Among contracts that could go to a returning supplier, 45\% do, about twice the rate implied by the strictest null and far above the rate implied by market structure alone. Much of this is the renewal of recurring services, as switching-cost theory predicts, but incumbents also win new needs more often than comparable firms. Incumbency is highest in health IT and under the exception route, and it coincides with single bidding. Market-level concentration is therefore an incomplete guide to competition in public procurement. Buyer-level incumbency, benchmarked against appropriate nulls and separated into renewals and new needs, shows where dependence arises.
