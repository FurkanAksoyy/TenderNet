\section{Data construction}

\subsection{Source and IT scope}
Award records were collected from EKAP (\texttt{ekapv2.kik.gov.tr}) by searching domain keywords. Search matches substrings of tender titles, which admits unrelated tenders. The keyword \emph{erp} retrieved 618 contracts, of which 546 are not IT (it matches, among others, the place name Yaverpaşa and \emph{tuz serpici}, a salt spreader). The phrase \emph{teknik destek} retrieved 1,186 contracts, of which 798 are hospital or university staffing and cleaning contracts. An earlier count-based cleaning had removed 394 obvious false positives from 13,418 records, leaving 13,024.

We re-audited this set by value. All 150 contracts with the highest value were classified by hand (108 IT, 1 call-centre service, 19 mixed, 22 not IT). For the remainder, ordered title rules assign each contract to one of four scope classes: IT, IT-enabled call-centre services, ambiguous, and not IT. Works contracts are ambiguous only if they install an IT or operational-technology system, such as city security cameras, SCADA, fibre, or a data centre, and are otherwise not IT. Staffing contracts for data entry are ambiguous; bundled cleaning or catering contracts without an IT system are not IT. Random samples from every rule were inspected, and seven rule outcomes for top contracts were overridden by hand. Table~\ref{tab:scope} reports the result. The main sample consists of the IT and call-centre classes.

EKAP's award list has one row per tender identifier, so tenders with several lots appear with a single winner. We found 116 main-sample titles that suggest multiple lots and no framework-agreement call-offs (the word \emph{çerçeve} appears only in the sense of ``within the framework of'').

\subsection{Firms, buyers, and markets}
\textbf{Firms.} Names were canonicalized by upper-casing with Turkish locale, removing punctuation and whitespace, and stripping legal-form suffixes from the end of the name, subject to a minimum remaining length. Joint ventures were kept separate from their members. The merge groups of the roughly 300 largest firms were reviewed by hand. Seventy-two brand groups were confirmed, including firms that changed legal form (for example from limited to joint-stock company) or name while keeping the same brand in non-overlapping years. Three uncertain brand pairs were left unmerged. The process reduces 4,788 recorded names to 4,389 firms. By a name-pattern heuristic, 536 of these firms (302 in the main sample) are natural persons. In the released data, every winner that is a natural person or lacks an explicit legal-entity form (690 firms) is replaced by a stable pseudonym.

\textbf{Buyers.} Thirty-six generic buyer labels pool units from two or more provinces, among them the provincial health directorates and hospital unions of the Ministry of Health. We split them by the province recorded for each tender (1,001 of the 13,024 contracts; 34 labels and 732 contracts in the main sample), which turns 3,453 labels into 3,663 buyer nodes. Six national bodies with offices in two provinces were not split. The recorded labels are used as a sensitivity check throughout.

\textbf{Buyer sectors.} Buyers were assigned to 11 sectors by ordered keyword rules on the buyer name. Relative to an earlier coding, 1,423 contracts changed sector, mainly because upper-casing without Turkish locale had prevented mixed-case names from matching, and because several rules matched unintended substrings.

\textbf{Product markets.} Contracts were assigned to 14 product markets by ordered rules on the tender title, most specific first; in the main sample, 97\% receive a specific market. To validate the rules, a random sample of 300 main-sample titles (seed 7) was labelled by three AI coders working blind to the rules: an earlier check and two independent coders based on different language models, each seeing only the titles and a codebook. The two independent coders agree on 88.7\% of titles ($\kappa=0.87$), and each agrees with the earlier check on 90--92\% ($\kappa=0.89$--0.91). The rules agree with the independent coders on 73--75\% of titles ($\kappa=0.70$--0.72), and with their joint label on 79.3\% of the 266 titles where the two coders agree. Against that joint label, health information systems has precision 1.00 and recall 0.99, GIS 1.00 and 0.94, and software licences 0.80 and 0.97. The weakest classes are custom software (0.48 and 0.56), maintenance (0.60 and 0.41), and the residual ``other IT'' class. The labels are AI-produced and have not been checked by human coders; blind coding sheets for two human coders are part of the replication material.

\textbf{Dates, procedures, values.} Dates are tender dates joined by tender identifier (coverage 100\%, October 2010 to March 2026). Procedures are open, restricted, and the negotiated procedures of Article~21(a)--(f); the extract contains no direct procurement under Article~22. Values are deflated to 2025 lira with the annual average of the consumer price index (2003=100; 2026 from the linked monthly series). With the domestic producer price index (Yİ-ÜFE) instead, count-based indices are unchanged. Seven single-variant value classifications move across the 1,000 or 1,800 boundary, and no robust classification changes.

\subsection{Article 21 of Law No.~4734}
Translations are ours. The chapeau reads: ``The negotiated procedure may be used in the following cases.''
\begin{itemize}
\item[(a)] No tender was submitted in an open or restricted procedure.
\item[(b)] Before 25 May 2018: the tender must be held urgently because of sudden and unexpected events such as natural disasters, epidemics, or a danger of loss of life or property, or of events the contracting authority could not foresee. Law No.~7144 (Art.~11, in force 25 May 2018) inserted: ``or in cases that have special features in terms of construction technique, or in which the contracting authority determines that the work must be carried out urgently to ensure structural safety or the safety of life and property''.
\item[(c)] The tender must be held urgently because of special defence- or security-related circumstances.
\item[(d)] The procurement requires research and development and is not subject to serial production.
\item[(e)] The technical and financial characteristics cannot be specified with the necessary precision because the goods, services, or works are original and complex.
\item[(f)] Purchases of goods, materials, or services whose estimated cost is below a monetary limit, updated every 1 February (for example 157,923 TRY in 2014 and 3,406,508 TRY in 2026).
\end{itemize}
In cases (b), (c), and (f), no notice needs to be published; at least three firms are invited to submit qualification documents and price offers. In our data all 2,957 main-sample 21(f) contracts are goods or services, and from February 2014 on, 99.96\% are below the limit in force.

\subsection{Nulls that preserve firm geography}
A firm's home province is the province where it won most contracts before the tender; firms without earlier wins form their own category. Null N4 permutes winners within market $\times$ year $\times$ winner's home province: the null mean is 0.173 (ratio 2.61, excess 1,370 contracts). Null N4b permutes within market $\times$ year $\times$ buyer province $\times$ winner's home province: the null mean is 0.264 (ratio 1.71, excess 923 contracts). Under N4b, 38.5\% of eligible contracts are in cells with a single possible winner, which makes it very conservative. Renewals have an incumbency of 0.720 against an N4b null of 0.391, new needs 0.305 against 0.195 (all $p<0.001$). A version with home provinces computed from all wins, including the contract itself, gives null means of 0.185 (N4) and 0.242 (N4b).

\subsection{Named-product licences and maintenance}
Titles that indicate licence renewal, maintenance, support, or updates of a named product (for example hospital information systems, Oracle, Microsoft) account for 10.5\% of repeat-eligible contracts. Their incumbency (0.486) is close to that of other contracts (0.448). Excluding them, renewals have an incumbency of 0.723 against an N3 null of 0.296 (2.44 times), and new needs 0.306 against 0.163 (1.88 times). Excluding all titles with any licence, maintenance, support, or update wording (44\% of eligible contracts) leaves ratios of 2.07 and 1.77.

\subsection{Renewals}
Titles are normalized by Turkish-aware lower-casing, removal of diacritics, digits, month names, and about 110 generic procurement words, and stemming to five characters; similarity is the Jaccard index of the stemmed word sets. A contract is a renewal if the buyer has a contract in the same market 183--548 days earlier with similarity of at least 0.5. Sixty best-match pairs, drawn at random within four similarity bands (15 each), were coded blind by the same AI coders. Of the 42 pairs above the threshold, 35--40 were judged the same recurring service (depending on the coder), but so were 6--10 of the 18 pairs below it. Agreement between the rule and the coders is moderate ($\kappa=0.40$--0.45), and between the two independent coders $\kappa=0.63$. The rule is thus precise but misses renewals whose titles were reworded, so the new-need group contains some renewals. Table~\ref{tab:renew} reports results by procedure and market and alternative definitions.

Figure~\ref{fig:buyers} shows the distribution of main-supplier shares. \section{Supplier choice and state dependence}
\textbf{Choice sets.} For each repeat-eligible contract, the alternatives are the firms that won at least one contract in the same product market in the 24 months strictly before the tender date; covariates use only information before the tender. In 38.7\% of the 4,913 repeat-eligible contracts the winner is not in this set (an entrant to the market or returning after more than 24 months): 65\% of non-incumbent wins, 20\% of renewals, 49\% of new needs, and 52\% of 21(f) contracts. The main model uses the remaining 3,013 contracts (909 buyers). At least one of the buyer's incumbents is in the choice set in 87.5\% of these contracts, and the most recent supplier in 85.8\%. An earlier version of the choice sets included firms whose only wins came after the tender. That produced an artificial ``no prior wins'' effect and is not used.

\textbf{Estimates.} Table~\ref{tab:clogit} reports the incumbent odds ratio across specifications. Adding entrant winners back to their choice sets gives a lower bound of 21.5 (18.5--25.0), because an entrant indicator cannot be estimated (it is one only for chosen alternatives). With a pool that includes firms active in any market, the incumbent odds ratio is 31.9 and firms new to the market have an odds ratio of 0.23.

\textbf{Future-supplier placebo.} We add an indicator for firms that supply the buyer in the market after the tender date but had not before. In the main specification its odds ratio is 54.4 (42.2--70.1), next to 63.7 for past suppliers; restricting to tenders up to 2023, to limit right-censoring of future ties, gives 52.4. The design inflates the future coefficient even without heterogeneity, because winning the current tender makes the winner an incumbent for later tenders. We therefore simulated 100 data sets with state dependence only. Winners of the 3,013 contracts are re-drawn in chronological order from the fitted main model, with incumbency recomputed from the simulated history. All other covariates keep their observed values, and entrant and first contracts keep their observed winners. The placebo model is then re-estimated on each simulated data set. The simulated future-supplier odds ratio is 19.8 (16.1--24.5), far below the observed 54.4, so persistent buyer--firm matching accounts for a substantial part of the incumbency effect. The comparison of past and future suppliers differs by type of purchase. For renewals the odds ratios are 258 and 128, for new needs 30 and 40.

\textbf{Firm-level versus dyadic heterogeneity.} Adding the firm's future wins in the same market from other buyers (odds ratio 1.40 per log unit) reduces the future-supplier odds ratio from 54.4 to 42.0 (32.3--54.5), and adding past wins from other buyers as well gives 41.5; for renewals it falls from 128 to 96 and for new needs from 40 to 31. Counting only future ties formed more than 30, 90, 180, or 365 days after the tender gives 56.1, 55.3, 52.7, and 48.7 (35.5 at 365 days with the firm controls).

The observed past/future ratio is below the state-dependence world in every sample (all $z=-6.6$, renewals $-4.9$, new needs $-7.9$; no simulated replication reaches the observed value). Against the heterogeneity world it is lower overall ($z=-2.9$) and for new needs ($-2.8$) but higher for renewals ($+2.5$). Heterogeneity alone produces a recency ratio above one, because a displaced supplier has revealed a worse match, so the last-versus-earlier contrast (1.69, 95\% CI 1.38--2.07, with a control for the number of past wins with the buyer) does not discriminate between the mechanisms.

\textbf{Intermediate worlds.} Fixing the incumbency coefficient at 0, 1, 2, or 3 and recalibrating $\sigma$ each time (50 replications), renewals fit between coefficients 2 and 3 (odds ratios 7--20): future-supplier odds ratio 183 and 125, past/future ratio 1.83 and 2.55, recency 2.35 and 2.00, against observed 128, 2.02, and 1.75. New needs fit best with no state dependence; their future-supplier odds ratio exceeds even the heterogeneity world, which suggests forward-looking ties such as multi-stage projects, or misspecification. The pooled sample fits no single combination because it mixes the two groups.

\section{Procedure and timing}

\subsection{Contract-level model}
The 21(b) $\times$ post-2018 odds ratio is 2.42 (1.43--4.09) with the buyer-history controls and two-way clustering, and 2.42 (1.57--3.73) with buyer clustering. Without the prior-contract and prior-supplier controls it is 2.90 (1.64--5.15); without any buyer-history controls it is 2.93 (1.65--5.19). The pre-2018 marginal effect of 21(b) is between $-3.5$ and $-3.8$ percentage points and insignificant in all specifications. Further checks give interaction odds ratios between 1.76 and 2.43: an IT-only sample, broad scope, recorded buyer labels, recorded firm names, a linear trend instead of year fixed effects, and excluding 2020. Alternative break dates give 2.55 (25 August 2017, KHK~694; log-likelihood $-2801.1$ against $-2802.5$ for 25 May 2018), 2.10 (placebo, May 2016, $p=0.009$), and 1.57 (placebo, May 2020, $p=0.08$). The interaction is 2.64 among health buyers and 0.86 ($p=0.78$) among other buyers. In a model with a triple interaction (two-way clustered), the post-2018 21(b) interaction is 2.44 ($p=0.003$) for health buyers and 0.89 for others. The difference is not significant (odds ratio 2.74, 95\% CI 0.79--9.51, $p=0.11$; in percentage points $+13.9$, $-7.6$ to 35.5), because only 84 non-health 21(b) contracts are repeat-eligible. Under Article~21(f), 34.6\% of repeat-eligible contracts go to an incumbent, against 13.1\% under N3 and 3.3\% under N1.

\subsection{Event study}
Figure~\ref{fig:event} reports the 21(b)--open gap by year relative to 2013--17. In that period the gap does not differ across years (joint tests $p=0.19$ for all buyers and $0.20$ for health buyers). In 2018 it is 37 percentage points higher for all buyers (24--50) and 43 points higher for health buyers (23--62). From 2019 on it averages 14 points above the reference for all buyers ($p=0.003$) and 12 points for health buyers ($p=0.04$). With buyer fixed effects, health buyers show a gap averaging $-10$ points in 2013--17, positive in every year from 2018, and 24 points (10--38) above the reference from 2019 on. Among parametric shapes, a step in August 2017 combined with a declining trend fits best. A step in May 2018 fits worse by 3.4--7.8 AIC points across the three samples, and a pure trend fits about as badly as no change. Only 8 and 10 repeat-eligible 21(b) contracts fall in the windows 25 August--31 December 2017 and 1 January--24 May 2018. The years 2010--12, with little buyer history and 24 21(b) contracts, show a high gap as well. Against that base, no later year is significantly higher.

\subsection{Dyad continuation}
For each new buyer--firm pair in a product market, we ask whether the pair signs another contract in that market. Of 7,761 pairs, 4,334 have no later contract by the buyer in that market and cannot continue. The analysis uses the remaining 3,427, of which 34.6\% continue. Covariates are measured at the first contract without look-ahead. The number of later opportunities (the buyer's later contracts in the market) enters as a covariate, because the offset restriction is rejected (coefficient 0.90 in Poisson pseudo-maximum likelihood, $p=0.02$; 0.88 in NB2, $p=0.006$). In NB2 the dispersion parameter is estimated ($\alpha=0.46$, SE 0.12; likelihood-ratio statistic for $\alpha=0$: 152.5). Firm size and prior distinct buyers are highly collinear ($r=0.99$; VIFs of 63 and 61), and generalism correlates 0.86 with size, so these variables are not separately identified in the full model. Entered alone, generalism and size are both positively associated with continuation. There is no evidence that specialist firms are more likely to keep a buyer. A first contract under 21(b) is associated with continuation (odds ratio 1.85).

\section{Single bidding}
\textbf{Random sample.} Bid counts were retrieved from published result announcements for a random sample of tenders stratified by year (40 per year drawn from all retrieved tenders). Of these, 393 are in the main sample and report bid counts; none is available for 2010--2011. Rates are weighted by the inverse sampling fraction of each year, with linearized 95\% intervals. Incumbency status is undefined when the tender is the buyer's first in the market. Adjusting for procedure and year (logit, buyer-clustered, $n=208$ tenders with defined incumbency), the odds ratio of a single bid for incumbent winners is 4.44 (2.39--8.25). Adding renewal status as a control gives odds ratios of 3.62 for a single bid and 2.95 for a single valid bid (2.63 without the renewal control). The single-bid rate has a weak upward trend (odds ratio 1.05 per year; $p=0.04$ unweighted, $p=0.11$ weighted). In 308 tenders with a recorded number of firms that obtained the documents, design-weighted averages are 4.0 such firms and 1.9 valid bids. In 45\% of these tenders two or more firms obtained the documents but only one valid bid was received.

\textbf{Software sample.} An interrupted enrichment run of the scraper retrieved result details for 3,400 tenders found with the first search keyword, \emph{yazılım} (software). For tenders from 2021 on, these include the number of valid bids. Joined to the main sample, 1,194 tenders have this count, about 94\% of software-titled tenders from 2021 on but only 35\% of all tenders in that period. The sample is therefore not random. Single valid bids occur in 66.7\% of incumbent-won tenders and 49.3\% of others. In a logit with procedure, year, and market fixed effects (681 tenders with defined incumbency, buyer-clustered), the incumbent odds ratio is 2.04 (1.40--2.96), and 2.68 when renewal status is added (renewal odds ratio 0.52). \section{Estimated costs and discounts}
\textbf{Sample.} From the 4,913 repeat-eligible contracts, 75 won by incumbents and 75 by other firms were drawn at random (seed 11). An AI agent opened each tender's detail page on EKAP in the authors' ordinary browser session, at a pace of roughly one tender per 40 seconds with pauses when the site slowed. It recorded the estimated cost, contract value, highest and lowest bids, product codes, and bid counts, without names, and its input list did not show incumbency. It did not interact with any verification widget. It collected 144 tenders before the site displayed a human-verification check, at which point collection stopped. One contract was in US dollars; 143 have both values in lira. EKAP contract values match our records within 1\% for 99.3\% of matched tenders.

\textbf{Discounts.} The discount is $1-\text{contract value}/\text{estimated cost}$. It averages 14.2\% (median 10.4\%, range $-9.9\%$ to 84.6\%).

\section{Network structure}
\textbf{Graph.} The main-sample graph has 2,814 firms, 2,890 buyers and 7,219 weighted edges, with density $8.9\times10^{-4}$. The giant component contains 4,446 nodes (78\%) and 92\% of contracts. Degree distributions (number of distinct partners) are heavy-tailed. Fitted by maximum likelihood with the protocol of Clauset, Shalizi and Newman (2009), firms have $\hat\alpha=2.21$, and a lognormal is not distinguishable from a power law ($R=-1.67$, $p=0.09$). For buyers ($\hat\alpha=2.06$) the power law is rejected by the bootstrap goodness-of-fit test, and a lognormal fits better ($R=-6.19$, $p<0.001$). Degree assortativity is $-0.17$ against $-0.04$ in the null. Bipartite clustering exceeds the null: Robins--Alexander 0.026 against 0.012, Latapy 0.31 against 0.25. Figure~\ref{fig:net} shows the core of the network.

\textbf{Communities.} Over 50 Louvain runs with random seeds and node orders, weighted modularity of the giant component ranges from 0.760 to 0.764 (34--43 communities). Barber's bipartite modularity of the same partitions is practically identical. In 200 bipartite degree-preserving randomizations (curveball), the unweighted modularity of the giant component is $0.694\pm0.003$ against an observed 0.731, and the weighted modularity is $0.756\pm0.003$ against an observed 0.761. Communities align with buyer sectors (NMI 0.25; permuted labels 0.03; purity 0.64 against a largest-label baseline of 0.44), and ten communities consist of at least 91\% health buyers. They align only weakly with firms' main product markets (NMI 0.13; permuted 0.04). In annual full graphs, modularity exceeds its bipartite null only modestly (annual $z$ between 0.4 and 3.9). Within annual giant components it does not exceed the null in 16 of 17 years, so we do not interpret yearly modularity as evidence of persistent segmentation.

\textbf{Projections and brokerage.} We computed exact Poisson-binomial $p$-values for co-occurrences under the bipartite configuration model (BiCM). With a false-discovery-rate correction over all pairs at 0.05 or 0.01, no link is validated in either projection, because the data are sparse: 94--97\% of co-occurring pairs share exactly one neighbour. A liberal variant that corrects only over co-occurring pairs retains 15,473 firm links and 42,005 buyer links. It essentially reproduces the raw projection without hub-mediated links, so we do not treat it as a validated backbone. Disparity-filter backbones of Newman-weighted projections at $\alpha=0.05$ contain 1,077 firm links (modularity 0.73) and 2,685 buyer links (0.73). They overlap little with the liberal BiCM set (Jaccard 0.04).

Across these projections and the raw projection, firm betweenness correlates with the number of product markets a firm serves, but also with its number of buyers. Controlling for buyers and contracts, the partial Spearman correlation is 0.12--0.15, and adding the number of markets to a size-only regression raises $R^2$ by 0.003--0.015. Brokerage thus adds little beyond firm size. In the raw buyer projection, the most connected buyer, a provincial health directorate once pooled labels are split, shares at least one supplier with 19\% of the projection's giant component. With recorded pooled labels the figure is 45\%, which shows how strongly such statistics depend on buyer coding.
